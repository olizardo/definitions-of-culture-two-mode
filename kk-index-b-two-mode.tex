\begin{abstract}
Kroeber and Kluckhohn's classic 1952 monograph \emph{Culture: A Critical Review of Concepts and Definitions} brought together over a hundred scholarly definitions of culture and indexed the key concepts they discuss. We build two networks from that index to explore how concepts and works are connected. In the \emph{definitions network}, concepts like ``society'' and ``beliefs'' are linked to 171 definition codes, grouped into seven categories and credited to the author or authors that K\&K index under each. In the \emph{works network}, the same concepts are linked to the books and papers cited in those definitions. By comparing both networks, we can see which concepts span many scholars' work and which are concentrated in particular sources. We present basic statistics about the networks, visualize the data using correspondence analysis and spanning trees, and highlight the key concepts and works that anchor each network.
\end{abstract}

\section{Data and networks}

We build two networks from K\&K's Index B. The \emph{definitions network} links 470 concepts---concrete ideas like ``society,'' ``customs,'' and ``beliefs''---to 171 definitions of culture, which K\&K number as codes. Each code is credited to the author or authors K\&K index under it, and falls into one of seven categories, which K\&K label with the capital letters A through G: A Descriptive, B Historical, C Normative, D Psychological, E Structural, F Genetic, and G Incomplete. The \emph{works network} links the same 470 concepts to 131 authored works (books, chapters, articles, and other publications), so we can see which ideas appear in which scholarly sources. Both networks use the same set of concepts, which lets us compare how the definitions and works overlap in what they discuss. Of the 171 codes, 169 appear in K\&K's own author index. The other two, C-I-18a and D-II-16, are attributions cited in the main text of the book that the index does not list. Boroch quotes two parts of four K\&K codes separately, lettered (a) and (b): A-3, E-5, F-II-4 and F-II-7. We keep each of these as two codes, A-3(a) and A-3(b) and so on, which is why the eight variant codes replace four. K\&K print one index entry per code, so each variant starts with its parent's concept links. For 29 of the 41 concept links copied in this way, we kept the link only on the variant whose quotation contains the concept's name; the other 12 remain on both variants. That assignment is our reading of the quotation text, not a printed fact. We leave out K\&K's Part III statement codes (C-5, D-5, D-7, E-17), which sit outside the Part II typology. Concepts have consistent names across the networks; where a concept appears under different headings in K\&K's source text, we treat it as a single concept. Works are named by their author(s) and year, and a single work can be cited under multiple definitions.

The definitions network naturally breaks into 10 separate components, with the largest containing 622 nodes. The works network has 10 components, with the largest containing 589 nodes. Our analysis focuses on these largest components, which contain virtually all the meaningful connections. We use maximum spanning trees across multiple projections to identify the strongest connections in different views of the network.

\section{Maximum spanning trees}

A maximum spanning tree (MST) keeps the strongest ties in a network while still connecting every node, using as few links as possible. A two-mode network has no ties between two concepts or between two definitions, so we analyze it through multiple one-mode projections \citep{everett2013dual-projection-c89}. Following Breiger \citeyearpar{breiger1974}, two concepts are tied if they share at least one definition or work, with weight equal to the number they share. Likewise, two definitions (or works) are tied through the concepts they share. In matrix form, the projections are:
\[
W_{\mathrm{concept}} = A A^{\top}, \qquad W_{\mathrm{def}} = A^{\top} A,
\]
where $(W_{\mathrm{concept}})_{ij} = \sum_k a_{ik} a_{jk}$ counts the definitions that include both concepts $i$ and $j$. Each projection is a weighted graph whose edges are the off-diagonal pairs with positive weight. We build projections from the 2-core of each network's largest component.

The maximum spanning tree of each projection is found by computing the minimum spanning tree on negated weights, $-w$. \citet{kruskal1956} sorts edges from largest weight to smallest and adds each unless it would close a cycle, producing a tree that maximizes $\sum_{e \in T} w_e$. Because the 2-core is connected, each tree has exactly $|V| - 1$ edges. In the visualizations, node size shows betweenness within the tree and node color shows the community structure detected using Newman's leading-eigenvector method applied to the tree.

The definition-level tree (Figure~\ref{fig:mst-def-code}) shows how 149 definitions cluster. Taken together, the four trees tell a consistent story. Five of the ten most central concepts are the same in both networks---group, social, behavior, society, and beliefs---and each of the leading concepts anchors one of the color regions of the concept trees, so the conceptual core of the culture concept is stable whether it is measured on definitions or on the works that cite them (customs, acquired, a people, activities, and learning enter the definitions ranking; ideas, art, morals, complex whole, and total enter the works ranking). The two networks differ in how concentrated their second mode is. Among works, centrality concentrates at a single point, Kelly \& Kluckhohn 1945a at 75\% of the maximum possible, with the runner-up at 61\%; the works literature is star-shaped around one synthetic review. Among definitions, the top code, C-I-6 at 71\%, is nearly as central, so the gap between the two networks is smaller than the earlier tree suggested; the definitions hub is a single Normative code rather than a review. In both concept trees the acquisition and transmission vocabulary sits on peripheral branches rather than at the core, suggesting that Genetic concerns, however common in the definitions themselves, connect to the rest of the conceptual structure only through a narrow path.

\section{Data-driven types: non-negative matrix factorization}

The spanning trees and the community colors above classify nodes hard: each work belongs to exactly one community of the tree. Non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) \citep{lee2001algorithms} offers a soft, parts-based alternative. It approximates a non-negative matrix $V$ by a product $W H$ with all entries non-negative, so every node carries a loading on each factor and every factor is an additive mixture of the original features, with no subtractive terms. We run two NMF analyses on the works network, one on the coding structure and one on the wording of the definition statements, and ask in each case how far the data-driven types agree with K\&K's own seven categories A--G. All use the same base-R implementation (Euclidean multiplicative updates, deterministic given a seed, with ten random restarts per value of $k$), and all fix $k$ at seven, one factor per K\&K group. Fixing $k$ this way is a choice, not a result: the reconstruction error falls monotonically in $k$ without an elbow, and restart agreement does not settle the choice either. For the works factorization it falls steadily with $k$ (mean adjusted Rand index 0.96 at $k = 3$, 0.55 at $k = 8$), and for the texts it is irregular (0.48 to 0.88 over $k = 3$ to $8$). Neither criterion selects a number of factors on its own.

\subsection{A spanning tree of the factor types}

The works factors can be hardened into a network the way the trees above were built. Binarizing the loadings at $0.10$ turns each work into a member of the factors it loads on substantially: 16 of the 118 works belong to a single factor, 51 to two, 41 to three, 9 to four, and one to five, and no work drops out. Projecting the resulting works $\times$ factors biadjacency ties two works by the number of factors they share, giving 4,383 ties in a single component, and the maximum spanning tree of that projection (Figure~\ref{fig:nmf-works-mst}) again has $|V| - 1$ edges. The threshold, like $k$, is a choice rather than a result: lower it and the projection densifies; raise it to $0.3$ and 71 of the 118 works fall back to a single factor, while one drops out entirely.

The tree's bridges are largely not the concept tree's. Kelly \& Kluckhohn 1945a, the dominant center of the concept-based work tree (Table~\ref{tab:mst-works-betweenness}), is a leaf here, tied to a single neighbor. In the other direction, Parsons 1949, the strongest bridge of the factor tree, is a leaf of the concept-based tree, and Osgood 1942 and Slotkin 1950, also central here, are leaves there. Winston 1933 and Linton 1936 are central in both trees, the only works in both top-ten lists. The two community partitions agree only weakly (adjusted Rand index 0.06), seventeen communities covering this tree against six in the concept-based one. Neither tree is wrong: the two tie definitions ask different questions. A work is central in the concept tree when it draws on many distinct sub-elements that other works also draw on; it is central in the factor tree when its pattern of concept use sits between the factor profiles of other works. The inversion is itself evidence for the reading below---the position of a work in the corpus depends on what is counted as a tie, and no tie definition so far recovers K\&K's groups.

\subsection{Types from the wording of the definitions}

The second analysis asks the same question of the definition texts themselves, independently of K\&K's coding. The corpus holds 171 definition codes carrying a quotation: the Table~2 texts mapped to K\&K codes through the crosswalk, the appendix-only texts, and one statement K\&K prints itself (a Part II page footnote). Documents average about thirty words, so the term--document matrix is built restrictively: tokens of three letters or more, a stopword list that removes the words nearly every definition contains (culture, society, social, man, and the general definitional vocabulary total, whole, learned, acquired), and terms kept to a document frequency between two documents and half the corpus, leaving 403 terms weighted by TF--IDF. The four quotations K\&K and Boroch print in French or German enter as English translations (K\&K's own rendering for Maquet, ours for the other three, verified against the printed originals). As with the coding structure, the factorization is run at $k = 7$, one factor per K\&K group.

The factors again find recognizable themes rather than K\&K's categories (Figure~\ref{fig:nmf-texts-factors}), and we label them by their leading terms: \emph{order and operation} (order, operation, self, superorganic, complex), \emph{feeling, thinking and acting} (inevitable, thinking, feeling, equipment, Kluckhohn), \emph{material vs.\ non-material} (material, non, traits, associated, uses), \emph{goals and persons} (certain, rather, others, persons, goals), \emph{symbolic interaction} (concrete, symbolic, process, through, interaction), \emph{species-specific ability} (events, species, use, dependent, ability), and \emph{mental and holistic} (concept, mean, holistic, traits, mental). On a corpus this small a factor can be driven by one author's phrasing---the literal word ``Kluckhohn'' is among the top terms of the feeling, thinking and acting factor---so the labels should be read as descriptive of the top terms, not as clean concepts. The labels for order and operation, feeling thinking and acting, symbolic interaction and species-specific ability are the least clear. Against the A--G groups the agreement is close to chance (adjusted Rand index 0.025, against a permutation 95th percentile of 0.016), with no factor exceeding 43\% purity in any group.

\subsection{Interpretation}

Neither signal recovers K\&K's taxonomy: not the works' pattern of concept use, not the wording of the definition statements. Two readings are possible. One is that K\&K's groups classify the emphases of individual \emph{statements}, and that these emphases are lost when statements are aggregated to the works that contain them; against this stands the fact that works overwhelmingly sit within a single group, so the aggregation mixes little. The other, which we find more plausible, is that the seven groups are editorial categories---useful for organizing a bibliography---rather than dimensions along which the corpus varies. This is consistent with the spanning-tree picture above, where the vocabulary the Genetic group concerns itself with sits on a peripheral chain of the concept trees rather than forming a region of its own, and with the community structure of the trees, which follows concept vocabulary, not authorial schools. Hardening the works factors into a tree (Figure~\ref{fig:nmf-works-mst}) does not change the verdict: its bridges are largely a different set of works, its communities do not follow A--G either, and the disagreement between the two tie definitions reinforces the point that no single structural reading of the corpus reproduces the taxonomy. The null result should be read against the method's limits as well: the number of factors was fixed to match the taxonomy under test rather than selected by the data, the short corpus makes TF--IDF factors fragile (restart agreement ranges from 0.48 to 0.88 for $k$ from 3 to 8), and the texts are K\&K's own selections rather than the works' full statements.

\section{Limitations}

The networks depend on the K\&K Index B transcription and on the verified-code filter. Element names are not unique across headings, so same-named elements are merged, which can join unrelated entries. Works are keyed by author and year, so a work with several year forms may appear twice. Several readings depend on OCR corrections recorded in the repository's manual-fix files; the validators check structure and round-trips, not the content of each edge. Two codes that are not in Index A are included: C-I-18a and D-II-16, from the main text. K\&K's Part III statements (C-5, D-5, D-7, E-17) are excluded. The basis for each is documented in the repository. The four split codes and the 29 link decisions that separate their variants (see the data section) are judgments: the variants share one K\&K index entry, and the text-based assignment of concept links is a reading that a reader can check against the quotations, not a printed fact. The definition-level trees and the text factors depend on these decisions.

The NMF comparisons in the body classify against K\&K's seven group letters. The two figures here repeat the comparison at the finer grain of K\&K's own typology, the roman-numeral subgroups the classification prints within groups C, D, and F; groups A, B, E, and G number their codes plainly and have no subgroup level.
