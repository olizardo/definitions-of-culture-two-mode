\begin{abstract}
Kroeber and Kluckhohn's landmark 1952 monograph, \emph{Culture: A Critical Review of Concepts and Definitions} \citep{kroeberkluckhohn1952}, represents the canonical mid-century effort to systematize the culture concept across anthropology and sociology. Beyond compiling over a hundred definitions, Kroeber and Kluckhohn indexed the substantive concepts comprising each definition and proposed an influential deductive taxonomy spanning seven broad categories. Rather than evaluating these definitions as isolated verbal pronouncements, I analyze the corpus as a two-mode network linking 171 definitions to 470 indexed concepts, exploiting the duality between definitions and concepts \citep{breiger1974}. Maximum spanning trees of the dual projections reveal that the conceptual space is anchored by a steep core-periphery architecture, where an ``ur-vocabulary'' of foundational concepts (\textbf{social}, \textbf{society}, \textbf{group}, \textbf{behavior}) provides common currency across theoretical boundaries. Structurally central definitions, most notably by Gillin and Bidney, achieve high betweenness through synthetic integration across normative, psychological, and genetic registers, whereas historically revered formulations like Tylor's occupy a specialized peripheral position. Finally, unsupervised latent Dirichlet allocation reveals that Kroeber and Kluckhohn's deductive taxonomy fails to capture the latent thematic organization of the actual texts, operating instead as an external editorial sorting scheme rather than an organic structure of mid-century culture theorizing.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

In 1952, anthropologists Kroeber and Kluckhohn published their comprehensive monograph, \emph{Culture: A Critical Review of Concepts and Definitions} \citep{kroeberkluckhohn1952}, hereafter K\&K. Published during a period of postwar disciplinary expansion and theoretical consolidation, the volume sought to establish intellectual order across anthropology, sociology, and social psychology by systematically assembling, comparing, and classifying every extant scholarly definition of culture. To tame this sprawling archive, K\&K devised a dual classification scheme. Inductively, they compiled an index of 470 constituent concepts or ``sub-elements'' (Index B) appearing across 171 definitions. Deductively, they sorted the definitions into seven overarching categories, spanning A Descriptive, B Historical, C Normative, D Psychological, E Structural, F Genetic, and G Incomplete.\footnote{K\&K further divide three of the seven categories into numbered subcategories, splitting Normative (C) into C-I and C-II, Psychological (D) into D-I through D-IV, and Genetic (F) into F-I through F-IV. The other four categories have no subcategories. Definitions that sit in a subcategory carry its numeral, as in C-I-18a or F-II-4 below.}

For decades, scholars have cited K\&K's compilation as evidence either of hopeless conceptual fragmentation or of an emergent consensus across the human sciences. Yet assessments of the volume have remained almost entirely qualitative, impressionistic, and centered on isolated textual excerpts. In this paper, I adopt a relational perspective, treating K\&K's archive not as a passive catalog but as a formal two-mode network connecting definitions to concepts \citep{breiger1974,everett2013dual-projection-c89}. The relational formulation rests on the principle of duality, where definitions are constituted by the specific configurations of concepts they mobilize, while concepts derive their disciplinary meaning and structural significance from the company of definitions in which they co-occur.

Treating the archive as a two-mode network allows an investigation of several fundamental questions in the cognitive history of social scientific concepts. First, what is the macro-topology of mid-century culture theorizing? Does the conceptual field exhibit a fragmented, modular structure composed of insular theoretical camps, or does it coalesce around a shared core-periphery architecture? Second, which specific definitions and concepts serve as structural bridges binding divergent intellectual traditions together? Third, does K\&K's deductive taxonomy reflect genuine latent boundaries in the actual wording of the definitions, or was their classification an artificial editorial schema imposed from above?

To answer these questions, I investigate both the structural backbone of the network using maximum spanning trees across dual one-mode projections and the latent semantic structure of the definition texts using latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA). The definitions network links 470 concepts to 171 definitions, with 169 definitions drawn from K\&K's primary author index and two (C-I-18a and D-II-16) identified from main-text citations. Following \citet{boroch2016}, four composite definitions are split into distinct quotations, yielding the 171 analyzed definitions.\footnote{Details regarding the transcription of Index B, OCR reconciliation, the variant splits, and edge resolution are documented in Appendix~\ref{app:construction}.} The full network contains 10 connected components, with the largest component containing 626 nodes (166 definitions and 460 concepts) and 1195 of the 1201 edges. The analyses that follow focus on the 2-core of this largest component, comprising 149 definitions and 160 concepts connected by 873 edges, which preserves all mutually reinforcing structural pathways while pruning isolated and pendant peripheral nodes.

In contrast to the skewed concept distribution, the definition degree distribution (right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:degree}) exhibits a symmetric, approximately bell-shaped profile centered around a mean of 7.02 concepts per definition (or 5.9 on the 2-core; median 5). The unimodal profile indicates that mid-century scholars adhered to a consistent norm of conceptual economy, where definitions were rarely minimalist single-concept formulas, nor were they boundless, undisciplined catalogs. Instead, the typical definition assembled between four and eight concepts, with the most expansive formulations topping out at 14 or 15 concepts (e.g., \citealt{reuter1939}; \citealt{taylor1948}; \citealt{hiller1933}; \citealt{gillin1942}).\footnote{Across all 171 definitions, quotation length correlates moderately with 2-core concept degree (Pearson $r = 0.55$, Spearman $\rho = 0.57$; $r = 0.60$ and $\rho = 0.61$ among the 149 definitions in the 2-core). When calibrated by quotation length, as shown in the rightmost columns of Table~\ref{tab:top-degree}, definitions by \citealt{dixon1928} (A-3(a)), \citealt{kluckhohn1942} (D-II-4), \citealt{young1934} (D-III-2), and \citealt{bain1942} (F-III-1) are exceptionally dense, packing over four distinct concepts into every ten words.}

The degree correlation between the two modes is slightly negative ($r = -0.076$), indicating a subtle disassortative tendency where peripheral concepts tend to attach to broad, multi-concept definitions, while peripheral definitions lean heavily on ubiquitous core concepts.\footnote{Against a degree-preserving null model that randomizes bipartite affiliations while preserving both marginal degree sequences (1000 draws; seed 6128), the expected correlation is $-0.033$ with standard deviation $0.022$ ($z = -1.94$, two-sided $p = 0.041$). The observed negative correlation is slightly stronger than degree constraints alone would dictate.} The relational dynamic is further illuminated by the nearest-neighbor degree statistics ($k_{nn}$). The average neighboring definition of an indexed concept contains 10.18 concepts, while the average neighboring concept of a definition appears in 9.22 definitions. Rather than splintering into isolated intellectual cliques, mid-century culture theorizing achieved macro-structural integration through a dual relational mechanism, where definitions gained breadth by linking to widely shared concepts, while concepts gained relevance by anchoring comprehensive definitions.

\section{Structural Backbones: Spanning Trees of the Dual Projections}

Because the bipartite affiliation matrix contains no direct ties between pairs of concepts or pairs of definitions, I examine the latent connections within each mode through dual one-mode projections \citep{breiger1974,everett2013dual-projection-c89}. Following standard practice, two concepts are tied if they co-occur within at least one definition, with the edge weight reflecting the number of shared definitions; dually, two definitions are tied through the shared concepts comprising them. In matrix notation, these dual projections are given by

\begin{equation}
W_{\mathrm{concept}} = A A^{\top}, \qquad W_{\mathrm{def}} = A^{\top} A,
\label{eq:projections}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $(W_{\mathrm{concept}})_{ij} = \sum_k a_{ik} a_{jk}$ represents the co-occurrence weight between concepts $i$ and $j$, and $(W_{\mathrm{def}})_{kl} = \sum_i a_{ik} a_{il}$ captures the conceptual overlap between definitions $k$ and $l$. Both projections are derived from the connected 2-core of the largest component (160 concepts and 149 definitions).

Dual one-mode projections are notoriously dense and redundant, making it difficult to discern their primary structural pathways \citep{neal2014backbone-b29}. To extract the underlying structural backbone of each projection, I compute its maximum spanning tree (MST). An MST identifies the minimally connected subgraph spanning all nodes that maximizes the total sum of retained edge weights ($\sum_{e \in T} w_e$), effectively filtering out dense cycles while preserving the strongest structural bridges \citep{kruskal1956}. Because tie weights frequently recur in discrete projection data, the maximum spanning tree is not mathematically unique. To guard against artifacts of arbitrary tie-breaking, I construct an ensemble of 1000 random maximum spanning trees for each projection, resolving weight ties in a randomized sequence using Kruskal's algorithm \citep{kruskal1956}. For each node, I compute its mean Freeman-normalized betweenness centrality across the 1000 trees \citep{freeman1978centrality-724}, along with the percentage of trees in which the node ranks in the top ten (the top-10 share).

\subsection{The Semantic Geography of the Concept Tree}

The concept backbone (Figure~\ref{fig:mst-def-sub}) reveals how 160 core concepts arrange themselves across the semantic landscape. Rather than a diffuse, undifferentiated web, the concept tree is structured around a small set of high-betweenness trunk nodes that mediate between distinct semantic regions. Across the ensemble, 21 concepts serve as terminal leaves in every single tree, and the median concept acts as a leaf in 76\% of trees. High degree is positively associated with tree betweenness (Spearman $\rho = 0.71$), yet degree alone does not determine structural centrality. Several concepts with 15 or more connections exhibit negligible betweenness. Specifically, \emph{total} (degree 22) achieves a mean betweenness of only 7.3\%, \emph{sum} (19) achieves 2.6\%, and \emph{transmission} (15) achieves a mere 0.9\%. By contrast, \textbf{ideas} (degree 21) registers 15.8\% betweenness, and \textbf{customs} (degree 16) commands 36.9\%, ranking fifth overall. While \emph{total} and \emph{sum} serve as generic syntactic qualifiers across many definitions, concepts like \textbf{customs} and \textbf{ideas} function as structural bridges linking distinct thematic domains.

Substantively, the concept backbone organizes mid-century culture discourse into four primary regional domains.

The first domain assembles the structural and systemic vocabulary, anchored by \textbf{group} (66.0\% betweenness), which forms the hub of an expansive community encompassing \emph{communication}, \emph{organization}, \emph{folkways}, \emph{systems}, \emph{designs for living}, and \emph{reactions}. The regional vocabulary reflects the structuralist and configurationalist emphasis on culture as patterned social organization.

The second domain carries the institutional and heritage vocabulary, anchored by \textbf{social} (57.8\% betweenness), which coordinates a cluster including \emph{social heritage}, \emph{institutions}, \emph{social needs}, \emph{patterns}, and \emph{human creation}. The region connects directly to \textbf{customs} (36.9\%), which heads a smaller branch containing \emph{accumulated treasury}, \emph{social group}, and \emph{tradition}.

The third domain gathers the behavioral and psychological vocabulary, anchored jointly by \textbf{society} (54.7\% betweenness) and \textbf{behavior} (51.6\% betweenness). The \textbf{society} hub organizes concepts referring to socialized actors (\emph{individuals}, \emph{shared}, \emph{basic needs}), while \textbf{behavior} links to cognitive and intentional dimensions (\emph{thought}, \emph{forms}, \emph{historically}, \emph{selection}, \emph{common ends}).

The fourth domain holds the normative and ideational vocabulary, mediated by \textbf{beliefs} (25.7\% betweenness), which links \emph{knowledge}, \emph{civilization}, and \emph{social procedures} to the rest of the tree, and by \textbf{ideas} (15.8\%), which heads a branch encompassing \emph{attitudes}, \emph{sentiments}, and \emph{values}. The intermediate concepts \textbf{habits} (19.3\%) and \textbf{members of a society} (21.5\%) serve as key structural conduits connecting ideational vocabulary to the behavioral \textbf{society} hub.

Notably, concepts related to cultural acquisition and transmission occupy a surprisingly peripheral branch of the backbone. Concepts such as \textbf{learning} (12.5\% betweenness), \emph{transmission}, \emph{socially}, \emph{products}, and \emph{persons} do not form an independent central axis; rather, they hang as a subordinate appendage off the \textbf{group} hub. This topological arrangement reveals an important feature of mid-century thinking about the culture concept, where theorizing focused primarily on characterizing culture as an established social-structural and behavioral entity, treating mechanisms of individual learning and transmission as secondary, derivative processes. In that respect, recent work in cognitive sociology has placed this previously peripheral emphasis at the center of cultural analysis \citep{lizardo2017improving-c29}.

\subsection{Bridging Definitions: Synthetic Theorizing vs. Historical Origin}

The definition-level tree (Figure~\ref{fig:mst-def-code}) displays the structural backbone connecting 149 definitions. High betweenness in this projection highlights definitions that act as theoretical bridges, binding together disparate conceptual vocabularies across the corpus. Seven of the top ten definitions in Table~\ref{tab:mst-def-betweenness} belong to K\&K's Normative (C) or Genetic (F) categories, while the remaining three (A-16, A-8, A-10) are Descriptive.

The single most prominent structural bridge in the entire corpus is Gillin's 1942 definition (C-I-6), commanding a mean betweenness of 77.2\% and appearing in the top ten across 100\% of the tree ensemble. Gillin's quotation sits at the center of the largest community in Figure~\ref{fig:mst-def-code}, linking definitions from six of K\&K's seven categories. Gillin's preeminent bridging status is not a trivial consequence of text length. At 64 words, the quotation sits at the 89th percentile of length, well below the longest definitions in the corpus (which reach 142 words). Rather, Gillin's centrality reflects a deliberate conceptual synthesis, weaving together customs, traditions, attitudes, ideas, symbols, social behavior, group patterns, and generational transmission into a single cohesive formulation. Accordingly, Gillin defines culture as follows:

\begin{quotation}
The \textbf{customs}, \textbf{traditions}, \textbf{attitudes}, \textbf{ideas}, and \textbf{symbols} which \textbf{govern} \textbf{social behavior} show a wide variety. Each \textbf{group}, each \textbf{society} has a set of \textbf{behavior patterns} (overt and covert) which are more or less \textbf{common} to the members, which are passed down from \textbf{generation} to \textbf{generation}, and \textbf{taught} to the \textbf{children}, and which are constantly liable to \textbf{change}. These \textbf{common} patterns we call the culture\ldots (\citealt{gillin1942})
\end{quotation}

Gillin's formulation coordinates every major conceptual register: the substantive contents of culture (customs, traditions, attitudes, ideas, symbols), the social and behavioral processes through which culture is enacted (governing social behavior, common to members), and the generational mechanisms through which it is transmitted (passed down, taught to children). While 93 other definitions share at least one of Gillin's core concepts, their average betweenness is only 4.7\%. Gillin's formulation achieves maximum structural prominence because it operates as a grand intersection, coordinating the normative, psychological, structural, and genetic vocabularies of mid-century social science.

An even more notable finding is the theoretical centrality of philosopher and anthropologist David Bidney \citeyearpar{bidney1968theoretical-29d}. Bidney accounts for three of the top six bridging definitions in the corpus, namely A-16 (ranking 2nd with 35.3\% betweenness), C-II-4 (4th with 29.1\%), and C-II-3 (6th with 26.9\%). Unlike sprawling descriptive inventories, Bidney achieves exceptional betweenness through concise, highly integrated formulations. Bidney's A-16 definition (\citealt{bidney1947}) is only 34 words long, yet it commands the second-highest betweenness in the network.

\begin{quotation}
[\ldots] functionally and secondarily, culture refers to the \textbf{acquired} forms of technique, \textbf{behavior}, \textbf{feeling} and \textbf{thought} of \textbf{individuals} within \textbf{society} and to the \textbf{social} \textbf{institutions} in which they \textbf{cooperate} for the attainment of \textbf{common ends}. (\citealt{bidney1947})
\end{quotation}

Bidney's formulation captures these same dimensions even more economically than Gillin, articulating the mechanisms of acquisition, the psychological constituents of culture (behavior, feeling, thought), the institutional matrix (individuals, society, social institutions), and the teleological function of culture (cooperation for common ends). Removing Bidney's A-16 definition from the representative tree shatters the network backbone into three disconnected components of 116, 31, and 1 definition. Bidney's other two central definitions, C-II-4 (40 words; 29.1\% betweenness) and C-II-3 (40 words; 26.9\% betweenness), share a distinct community that explicitly synthesizes psychological and normative registers.

\begin{quotation}
An integral or holistic concept of culture comprises the \textbf{acquired} or \textbf{cultivated} \textbf{behavior}, \textbf{feeling}, and \textbf{thought} of \textbf{individuals} within a \textbf{society} as well as the \textbf{patterns} or \textbf{forms} of intellectual, social, and artistic ideals which human societies have professed \textbf{historically}. (\citealt{bidney1946})
\end{quotation}

\begin{quotation}
A culture consists of the \textbf{acquired} or \textbf{cultivated} \textbf{behavior} and \textbf{thought} of \textbf{individuals} within a \textbf{society}, as well as of the intellectual, artistic, and social ideals which the members of the society profess and to which they strive to conform. (\citealt{bidney1942})
\end{quotation}

Bidney's structural centrality reveals a key dimension of mid-century concept formation around culture. In standard disciplinary histories, Bidney is often remembered as an eccentric philosophical critic of Boasian culture theory. Yet within the relational topology of the conceptual network, Bidney emerged as the preeminent synthetic theorist of the era. His self-conscious quest for an ``integral or holistic'' concept of culture led him to craft definitions that systematically reconciled individual psychological processes (acquired behavior, feeling, thought) with collective social facts (institutions, ideals, historical patterns) and more ``normative'' conceptions of the values and goals instilled by the enculturation process.

Processual and genetic definitions also occupy prominent bridging positions. Folsom's 1931 definition (F-I-3; 75 words) ranks 3rd with 33.3\% betweenness. The formulation is distinctive in its attempt to bridge material artifacts and social ideals, emphasizing the totality of human production and the generational transmission of culture.

\begin{quotation}
Culture is not any part of man or his inborn equipment. It is the sum total of \textbf{all that man has produced}: \textbf{tools}, \textbf{symbols}, most organizations, \textbf{common} \textbf{activities}, \textbf{attitudes}, and \textbf{beliefs}. It includes both \textbf{physical products} and \textbf{immaterial products}. It is everything of a \textbf{relatively permanent} character that we call \textbf{artificial}, everything which is \textbf{passed down} from one generation to the next rather than \textbf{acquired} by each generation for itself: it is, in short, \textbf{civilization}. (\citealt{folsom1931})
\end{quotation}

Similarly, Murdock's 1949 formulation (F-I-19; 70 words) ranks 5th with 28.5\% betweenness, explicitly framing culture as the synthetic outcome of the ``interaction of learning and society.''

\begin{quotation}
The \textbf{interaction} of \textbf{learning} and \textbf{society} thus produces in every human \textbf{group} a \textbf{body} of \textbf{socially transmitted} \textbf{adaptive behavior} which appears super–individual because it is \textbf{shared}, because it is \textbf{perpetuated} beyond the individual life span, and because its quantity and quality so vastly exceeds the capacity of any single person to achieve by his own unaided effort. The term culture is applied to such \textbf{systems} of \textbf{acquired} and transmitted \textbf{behavior}. (\citealt{murdock1949a})
\end{quotation}

In contrast to these central synthesizers, other prominent definitions occupy surprisingly peripheral structural positions. Kroeber's own definition (A-17; 65 words) ranks 25th in tree betweenness (5.7\%), functioning as a local descriptive summary rather than an integrative global bridge.

\begin{quotation}
[\ldots] the \textbf{mass} of learned and transmitted \textbf{motor reactions}, \textbf{habits}, \textbf{techniques}, \textbf{ideas}, and \textbf{values} -- and the \textbf{behavior} they induce -- is what constitutes culture. Culture is the special and exclusive product of men, and is their distinctive quality in the cosmos [\ldots]. Culture [\ldots] is at one and the same time the \textbf{totality} of products of social men, and a tremendous \textbf{force} affecting all human beings, \textbf{socially} and \textbf{individually}. (\citealt{kroeber1948a})
\end{quotation}

Even more instructive is the structural position of Tylor's foundational 1871 definition (A-1), widely canonized as the historical origin of the anthropological concept of culture (and thus placed first in K\&K's Index B).

\begin{quotation}
Culture, or \textbf{civilization}, [\ldots] is that \textbf{complex whole} which includes \textbf{knowledge}, belief, \textbf{art}, law, \textbf{morals}, custom, and any other \textbf{capabilities} and \textbf{habits} \textbf{acquired} by man as a member of \textbf{society}. (\citealt{tylor1871})
\end{quotation}

Tylor's definition links to 12 concepts in the 2-core (\textbf{society}, \textbf{customs}, \textbf{beliefs}, \textbf{members of a society}, \textbf{habits}), yet it achieves a mean betweenness of only 20.2\%, ranking 11th overall. In Figure~\ref{fig:mst-def-code}, Tylor sits within an eight-definition cluster on the periphery of the network backbone, largely decoupled from the central bridges occupied by Gillin and Bidney. The peripheral position reveals a critical divergence between historical veneration and structural integration; while Tylor's formula provided the discipline of anthropology with its founding formulation, twentieth-century culture theorizing moved via synthetic bridges that integrated behavioral, psychological, and normative registers, leaving Tylor's classic definition as an honored but specialized peripheral outpost.

\section{The Ground-Up Semantics of Culture Definitions}

While K\&K's Index B provides an inductive mapping of constituent concepts, K\&K's primary organizational intervention was deductive, dividing definitions into seven broad categories (A Descriptive, B Historical, C Normative, D Psychological, E Structural, F Genetic, and G Incomplete). Did these categories capture genuine latent semantic boundaries in how scholars formulated definitions, or were they an external taxonomic grid imposed upon the texts by the authors?

To address this question without relying on K\&K's manual index or predefined categories, I turn to latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA) \citep{blei2003latent} a relatively straightforward approach to text classification. LDA models each text as an unsupervised mixture over latent ``topics'' (latent variables), where each topic is characterized by a multinomial distribution over terms. The distribution of words over texts in the corpus is assumed to be indepenndent conditional on topic. 

To respect the multi-clause nature of complex definitions, I segment each quotation at sentence and semicolon boundaries and treat the individual clause as the unit of topic estimation. The definition itself remains the unit of analysis, since clause-level topic memberships are aggregated back to definitions before any comparison with K\&K's categories. Filtering out clauses with fewer than three words, removing stopwords, meta-linguistic terms (such as \emph{definition}, \emph{concept}, and the \emph{define}, \emph{include}, and \emph{designate} verb stems), and high-frequency frame words (\emph{culture}, \emph{society}, \emph{social}, \emph{man}, \emph{men}), joining adjacent multiword units that repeat across definitions (\emph{sum total} and \emph{complex whole}), and collapsing plurals to their singular forms produced an initial corpus. Retaining terms appearing in at least two clauses yielded a final modeled corpus of 264 clauses across 169 definitions and a vocabulary of 334 terms.\footnote{Two definitions, C-I-7 (\citealt{simmons1942}) and G-1 (\citealt{sapir1921}), contained no term surviving the frequency filter and were assigned uniform topic distributions; they are omitted from the category comparison and ranking.}

Following standard validation procedures, I fit an LDA model with $k = 12$ topics using Gibbs sampling, setting a sparse Dirichlet document-topic prior of $\alpha = 0.1$ to encourage clear, well-differentiated topic assignments \citep{griffiths2004finding}.\footnote{Held-out perplexity evaluated across $k = 8$ to $30$ remains essentially flat, indicating that $k = 12$ provides an optimal balance between granular semantic interpretability and model parsimony.} Because topic models estimated through stochastic sampling are prone to arbitrary label switching across runs, the theme indexed as Topic 1 in one estimation run might appear as Topic 5 or Topic 9 in another. Simply averaging raw topic weights across independent runs would therefore blur distinct semantic registers into an undifferentiated composite. To guard against sampling variability while preserving coherent thematic boundaries, I estimate 50 independent runs and align their topics to a shared reference fit using the Hungarian algorithm, an optimal one-to-one matching procedure. For each run, the algorithm computes the semantic similarity (the cosine similarity of term distributions) between every topic pair and finds the unique assignment that maximizes total vocabulary overlap with the reference model. Counterparts across the 50 runs correspond closely, yielding an average alignment similarity of 0.70. Once topics are harmonized under shared identities, each definition's final topic weights represent the clause-length-weighted average across all 50 aligned runs.

Across the twelve latent registers, mid-century theorists advanced four distinct conceptual models of culture. The first cluster preserves the classical ethnographic tradition of defining culture holistically as an aggregate property of a bounded collective. In \emph{customs, beliefs and knowledge} (Topic 1), Dixon's formulation (A-3a) treats culture enumeratively as the ``sum of all activities, customs, and beliefs,'' embodying the Boasian trait-inventory format without imposing functional or psychological integration. In \emph{way of life of a people} (Topic 4), Kluckhohn and Leighton (C-I-13) articulate the relativistic ethnographic idiom of the 1940s, defining culture as a collective's distinctive ``way of life.'' Both formulations approach culture from the outside, conceptualizing it as an encompassing, descriptive totality tied to a specific group without probing internal cognitive or psychological mechanisms.

The second cluster conceptualizes culture as an autonomous, emergent superorganic stratum unique to human beings. In \emph{distinct order of human phenomena} (Topic 5), Ostwald (F-IV-2) exemplifies the early twentieth-century superorganic perspective, defining culture not as a catalog of behavior but as the evolutionary threshold that sets the human species apart from other animals. Leslie White (F-III-2) grounds this emergent level in his ``culturological'' theory of \emph{material tools and ideas} (Topic 8), uniting physical artifacts and mental ideas under the singular human capacity for ``symbolling.'' In \emph{shared designs for living} (Topic 3), Sorokin (C-II-6) formalizes this macro-sociological orientation, distinguishing the relational network of interacting individuals from the cultural realm of meanings, values, and norms that coordinates group life.

The third cluster reflects the behaviorist and operationalist effort in mid-century American social science to ground culture in observable transmission and environmental modification. In \emph{transmitted group behavior} (Topic 2), Benedict (D-II-6) defines culture strictly through extra-somatic social learning, explicitly contrasting human intergenerational transmission with insect instinct to demarcate learned behavior from biological inheritance. In \emph{human products and results} (Topic 12), the 1941 Committee on Conceptual Integration of the American Sociological Society (D-I-9)\footnote{The Committee on Conceptual Integration (C.C.I.) was organized by the American Sociological Society (now the American Sociological Association) to establish standardized operational terminology across the discipline. At the 36th Annual Meeting held at the Roosevelt Hotel in New York City (December 27--29, 1941), the committee presented two dedicated sessions, including a panel on ``The Report of the C.C.I. Sub-Committee on the Definitions of Culture'' featuring Bronislaw Malinowski, L.~L. Bernard, F.~Stuart Chapin, and Hornell Hart, alongside a session on systematization with Stuart C. Dodd and Talcott Parsons (see the 1941 Annual Meeting Program, \url{https://www.asanet.org/wp-content/uploads/1941_annual_meeting_program_0.pdf}).} operationalizes this behavioral view into an empirical formula, defining culture as the tangible end-products of learned adjustment efforts. Willey (F-I-1a) complements this operationalism in \emph{man-made environment} (Topic 9) by conceptualizing culture as an objective, cumulative physical and institutional environment that confronts the individual from the outside, requiring ongoing adjustment.

The fourth cluster situates culture at the interface of individual psychology, biological needs, and normative habits. Panunzio (A-11) in \emph{biological needs and nature} (Topic 10) articulates a Malinowskian bio-cultural functionalism, interpreting culture as an instrumental apparatus organized to satisfy fundamental biological and physical needs. Kluckhohn and Leighton (D-I-12) in \emph{problem-solving ways of thinking} (Topic 7) advance an adaptive model rooted in psychological functionalism, defining culture as a repertoire of habitual, historically tested solutions to recurrent human dilemmas. Kluckhohn and Kelly (C-I-10) in \emph{mental constructs of individuals} (Topic 6) translate this into an internal cognitive filter, defining culture as historically created selective processes that channel reactions to internal and external stimuli. Finally, Bidney (C-II-3) in \emph{acquired habits of individuals} (Topic 11) resolves the tension between individual psychology and collective order by uniting cultivated habits of thought and behavior with professed social ideals, providing a psychological-normative bridge that avoids both mechanical behaviorism and disembodied superorganic abstraction.

The distribution of definitions across Table~\ref{tab:lda-top-definitions} reveals several foundational patterns in the structure of mid-century culture theorizing. Topologically, the twelve latent topics bifurcate into two distinct organizational modes: specialized thematic pillars and synthetic crossroads. On the one hand, two topics act as insular thematic pillars monopolized by a single administrative category. \emph{Customs, beliefs and knowledge} draws all five of its leading definitions from Category A (Descriptive), capturing the traditional Boasian trait-inventory format. Similarly, \emph{way of life of a people} draws all five of its leading definitions from Category C-I (Normative), isolating the relativistic, holistic idiom characteristic of 1940s anthropology. On the other hand, the most dynamic theoretical arenas function as synthetic crossroads, drawing formulations from across four or five different K\&K categories into the same latent semantic register. \emph{Shared designs for living} unites normative, structural, psychological, and historical definitions; \emph{material tools and ideas} spans genetic, normative, descriptive, and psychological categories; and \emph{acquired habits of individuals} coordinates normative, descriptive, historical, and structural entries.

Looking at individual authors underscores the institutional mechanisms driving this conceptual synthesis. Most prominent is Clyde Kluckhohn, who (either solo or co-authored with Kelly and Leighton) occupies eight of the sixty top positions across four distinct topics (\emph{shared designs for living}, \emph{way of life of a people}, \emph{mental constructs of individuals}, and \emph{problem-solving ways of thinking}). Kluckhohn was not merely an encyclopedic compiler alongside Kroeber; his own postwar formulations operated as the primary institutional crossroads of the discipline, self-consciously deploying multiple semantic registers to forge a synthetic theoretical consensus. A contrasting collective dynamic appears in the 1941 Committee on Conceptual Integration (Bernard, Blumenthal, Dodd, and Hart), which captures three of the top five ranks in \emph{human products and results} (D-I-9, F-I-9, F-I-10) and recurs in \emph{distinct order of human phenomena} (F-I-11). The committee operated as a deliberate positivist vanguard, coordinating their definitions around an operationalist, behaviorist vocabulary designed to strip the culture concept of metaphysical ambiguity.

The distribution also reveals which conceptual idioms had receded by mid-century. Category B (Historical) definitions appear only twice in the entire table (B-5 by Bose in Topic 3 and B-8 by Lowie in Topic 11), while Category G (Incomplete) definitions are entirely absent. By 1952, defining culture merely as ``tradition'' or ``social heritage'' had ceased to represent an active theoretical frontier; substantive debate had moved toward articulating specific mechanisms of social learning, symbolic mediation, and individual habituation. Finally, the asymmetry in peak topic weights highlights a fundamental difference in conceptual composition. Topics like \emph{biological needs and nature} (Panunzio at 0.87) and \emph{acquired habits of individuals} (Bidney at 0.83) are anchored by crisp, single-register archetypes. Conversely, topics such as \emph{shared designs for living} (peaking at 0.39) and \emph{man-made environment} (peaking at 0.45) exhibit much lower maximal weights, indicating that these were inherently composite notions that theorists could not articulate without simultaneously invoking multiple semantic registers.

\subsection{Deductive Taxonomy versus Latent Thematic Structure}

Do K\&K's seven deductive categories correspond to the bottom-up thematic structure revealed by LDA? A cross-tabulation of each definition's dominant topic against its K\&K category yields a statistically significant association ($\chi^2 = 121.1$, simulated $p < 0.001$ across 10,000 replications), indicating that topic distributions are not independent of category assignments.\footnote{Expected cell frequencies fall below 5 in 80 of the 84 table cells, precluding standard asymptotic chi-square approximations; the reported significance level is derived via Monte Carlo permutation under the null hypothesis of independence. This pattern is not an artifact of model specification. Refitting the clause corpus with a biterm topic model \citep{yan2013biterm}, which pools word co-occurrences across the entire corpus rather than modeling word occurrences within documents, returns a significant association across 20 independent fits ($\chi^2$ between 89 and 135, simulated $p \le 0.029$) and the same near-zero agreement with K\&K's groups (mean ARI $= 0.033$). The biterm model concentrates definitions more sharply on their dominant topic (median dominant weight 0.62 against 0.36 under LDA), showing that the magnitude of mixture is partly a feature of the estimator.} Yet this departure from pure randomness does not imply that K\&K's categories represent cohesive semantic clusters.

The Sankey diagram in Figure~\ref{fig:sup-sankey-codes} maps the flows between K\&K's deductive categories (and subgroups) on the left and dominant LDA topics on the right. Rather than a clean, block-diagonal mapping where deductive classes match latent semantic clusters, the diagram reveals pervasive cross-boundary dispersion.

Examining the flows from the category side clarifies how K\&K's deductive taxonomy fractured thematically. Rather than representing coherent semantic clusters, K\&K's categories grouped definitions by external administrative criteria and putative disciplinary provenance. Psychological (D) definitions, the largest category in the corpus ($N = 39$), disperse across all twelve latent topics. This fragmentation reflects the conceptual heterogeneity of postwar psychological discourse, which simultaneously encompassed Hullian behaviorist learning theory (Topic 2), Malinowskian drive-satisfaction (Topic 10), pragmatic situational adjustment (Topic 7), and individual habit formation (Topic 11). Similarly, Genetic (F) definitions ($N = 43$), which K\&K sorted under the broad heading of how culture originates, disperse across eleven topics. Under the single umbrella of genesis, K\&K conflated the physical fabrication of material tools (Topic 8), the creation of an objective anthropic environment (Topic 9), the institutional end-products of social labor (Topic 12), and the emergent evolutionary threshold separating humanity from animals (Topic 5). Normative (C) definitions split along comparable fault lines, separating holistic group-level styles of life (Topic 4) from individual normative conformity and moral ideals (Topic 11).

The view from the topic side reinforces this diagnosis. While a few topics represent specialized traditional niches---most notably \emph{customs, beliefs and knowledge}, which remains the insular preserve of Category A descriptive trait inventories, and \emph{distinct order of human phenomena}, dominated by Category F evolutionary formulations---the primary theoretical bridges in the network draw indiscriminately across the taxonomy. Most strikingly, \emph{acquired habits of individuals} draws definitions from all seven K\&K categories, while \emph{shared designs for living} unites normative, structural, psychological, and historical entries. At the level of individual formulations, the median definition allocates only 36\% of its total probability mass to its primary topic, compared to a baseline uniform distribution of 8.3\%. Rather than adhering to pure types, mid-century definitions were composite mixtures combining multiple semantic domains.

\section{Discussion}

In this paper, I have re-examined Kroeber and Kluckhohn's 1952 compendium of culture definitions through the dual lenses of bipartite network analysis and natural language processing. Rather than treating their compilation as a static museum of historical artifacts, approaching the archive through the principle of duality \citep{breiger1974} uncovers the latent structural and semantic organization of mid-century culture theorizing. Four broad substantive implications emerge from this investigation.

First, the findings show the analytical value of relational duality for the cognitive history of social scientific concepts. Standard historiographies of social science typically evaluate concepts through the lens of individual intellectual biographies or broad, reified paradigm shifts. By formalizing the definitional archive as a two-mode affiliation network, I show how concepts and theoretical statements mutually constitute one another. Concepts do not exist as isolated lexical units; they achieve empirical traction, disciplinary consensus, and institutional durability because they are coordinated across competing definitions. Reciprocally, definitions achieve synthetic scope and structural influence because they assemble specific configurations of shared concepts. The bipartite formulation moves beyond impressionistic textual exegesis, providing a rigorous analytical methodology for mapping how scientific lexicons stabilize and evolve through relational affiliation.

Second, the macro-topology of mid-century culture theorizing resolves a long-standing disciplinary puzzle regarding consensus and fragmentation. Commentators have frequently vacillated between viewing mid-century culture theory as a chaotic babble of incompatible voices or as an incipient cross-disciplinary consensus. The network structure shows how both impressions arose simultaneously from a single underlying topology. At its center, the conceptual space is anchored by a steep, heavy-tailed ``ur-vocabulary'' centered on collective social life (\textbf{social}, \textbf{society}, \textbf{group}, \textbf{behavior}, \textbf{ideas}, \textbf{customs}). The shared core provided an invariant common currency across ostensibly rival theoretical camps, from Boasian diffusionism and Malinowskian functionalism to Hullian behaviorism. At the same time, definitions adhered to a disciplined economy of expression, typically combining four to eight concepts by pairing a modest selection of core anchor terms with specialized peripheral notions. Disciplinary consensus and localized theoretical innovation were therefore structural complements; the core preserved collective intelligibility, while the periphery permitted open-ended conceptual differentiation.

Third, examining the structural backbone alongside the unsupervised topic models highlights the preeminent importance of \emph{synthetic theorizing} in scientific concept formation. In network terms, structural centrality does not belong to voluminous descriptive catalogs or narrow polemical manifestos; it belongs to integrative formulations that span divergent conceptual registers. Gillin (1942) and Bidney (1942, 1946, 1947) emerge as the paramount structural bridges in the corpus because they self-consciously formulated definitions that reconciled individual psychological habits and behavioral learning with normative ideals and collective social structures. Bidney's prominent role is particularly instructive. While standard intellectual histories often marginalize Bidney as an idiosyncratic philosophical critic of anthropology, topologically his holistic formulations served as the primary relational conduits binding the discipline together.

The topic modeling results directly reinforce this synthetic dynamic. Topologically, the twelve latent semantic registers bifurcate into insular thematic pillars and dynamic synthetic crossroads. While traditional trait inventories (\emph{customs, beliefs and knowledge}) and relativistic life-ways (\emph{way of life of a people}) remained insular pillars monopolized by single administrative categories, the most active conceptual arenas---\emph{shared designs for living}, \emph{material tools and ideas}, and \emph{acquired habits of individuals}---functioned as synthetic crossroads drawing definitions from across four or five distinct K\&K categories. Clyde Kluckhohn's institutional ubiquity across four separate latent topics further illustrates how leading figures forged consensus by acting as multi-register synthetic crossroads. A contrasting collective strategy appears in the ASS's 1941 Committee on Conceptual Integration, which operated as an operationalist cartel seeking to standardize the discipline around behavioral end-products. In sharp contrast to these synthetic efforts, ceremonial origin formulations like Tylor's 1871 definition occupy peripheral outposts in both the network backbone and topic space, revealing a clear divergence between ceremonial historical veneration and actual structural integration in scientific discourse.

The analysis shows that K\&K's deductive administrative taxonomy failed to capture this generative synthesis. When tested against the verbatim text, K\&K's seven-category taxonomy fails to recover the natural thematic structure of mid-century theorizing. K\&K's taxonomy was an external editorial apparatus designed to organize an extensive bibliographic review, not an organic representation of how practicing social scientists conceptualized culture. By sorting definitions according to external institutional provenance or surface terminology, K\&K grouped radically different mechanisms under single labels, conflating drive-reduction, habituation, and cognitive filtering under the ``Psychological'' heading, and combining tool-making, anthropic ecology, and superorganic evolutionary emergence under the ``Genetic'' rubric.

Finally, the investigation carries broader stakes for computational concept analysis, cognitive sociology, and the historical trajectory of social science. Methodologically, the findings offer a cautionary lesson for contemporary computational social science and natural language processing. Computational studies of scientific and cultural fields frequently take formal administrative classifications---such as library subject headings, journal categories, or patent classes---as ground-truth representations of intellectual domains. The analysis shows that top-down administrative schemes often obscure bottom-up semantic connections. Mid-century definitions were pervasive thematic mixtures, with the median definition allocating only 36\% of its weight to its dominant topic. Evaluating scientific texts through unsupervised topic mixtures and relational networks provides a far more faithful representation of the composite, multi-register nature of theoretical concepts.

Substantively, the analysis illuminates an important evolutionary shift in the culture concept itself. In the topic models, Category B (Historical) definitions recede to near-invisibility, appearing only twice among top definitions, while Category G (Incomplete) vanishes entirely. By mid-century, treating culture as passive ``tradition'' or ``social heritage'' had ceased to represent an active theoretical frontier; substantive inquiry had turned toward explicit mechanisms of social learning, symbolic mediation, cognitive filtering, and individual habituation. In that respect, mid-century theorists were already grappling with the fundamental duality of culture as an objective public environment and as an internalized cognitive apparatus. While mid-century social scientists lacked the contemporary cognitive langauage of dual-process cognition, cultural schemas, and embodied habitus \citep{lizardo2017improving-c29}, their synthetic formulations foreshadowed this exact theoretical development. Rather than representing an era of terminal terminological confusion, mid-century culture theorizing constituted an active, generative stage in the cognitive history of social science, constructing the structural and semantic bridges that continue to organize contemporary cultural analysis.

\section{Constructing the definition-level data}\label{app:construction}

The analyses rely on a single primary data structure, a table containing one row for each of the 171 K\&K definitions. Each row specifies the definition's group and subgroup, its constituent sub-elements, and the citation identifying its quotation. Here I record how I built that table, the methodological choices made along the way, and the empirical basis for each decision. I join two primary sources. K\&K's Index A and Index B, extracted from an OCR scan of the 1952 monograph, provide each definition's author, year, and sub-elements. Boroch's \citeyearpar{boroch2016} article supplies each definition's quotation along with page pinpoints. Every join follows an explicit rule, and I document the rationale for each judgment in the study's provenance records.

\subsection{Sources and the unit}

The unit of analysis is the individual definition, corresponding to one printed index entry in K\&K and one quotation in \citet{boroch2016}. Boroch's appendix contains 171 definitions. Boroch's Table 2 has 135 columns, 132 of which match an appendix definition. The two sources number definitions differently. Boroch renumbers within each group, so that his A10 (\citealt{winston1933}) corresponds to K\&K's A-9. I therefore match definitions by author, year, group, and subgroup, retaining positional agreement strictly as a diagnostic check. Of the 100 matches established by author and year, 48 agree with the positional ordering.

\subsection{Index A and Index B}

I extracted Index A (authors) and Index B (sub-elements) from the OCR text layer and corrected them using explicit rules. I document these corrections in a manual-fix table of 142 rows, each verified against printed-page evidence. A further 10 rows recover definitions that OCR processing merged into element names, and 14 rows record author-name disambiguations. I avoid general character-substitution rules, because heuristic replacements (such as mapping `y' to `5') silently generate spurious definitions from unrelated tokens. Each manual fix is keyed to a parsed token, and automated checks confirm that every key matches an existing token.

The target universe comprises 171 definitions. Of these, 169 appear in K\&K's author index. The remaining two, C-I-18a (\citealt{maquet1949}) and D-II-16 (\citealt{aberle1950}), are cited in main-text footnotes but omitted from the author index; I recover their Index B affiliations directly from the sub-element listings that reference them. Four Part III statement definitions (C-5, D-5, D-7, and E-17) are excluded, because they represent K\&K's own analytical statements rather than Part II definitions.

\subsection{Matching Boroch's definitions to K\&K's}

The crosswalk matches each column of Boroch's Table 2 to a K\&K definition. Of the 135 columns, 117 match automatically when exactly one definition satisfies the criteria, and 15 more are resolved through manual inspection, each documented with its empirical rationale. Three of Boroch's Table 2 columns have no appendix entry of their own and carry no K\&K definition, namely A10a, C1-5a, and F4-6. Each is a verbatim duplicate of another quotation, leaving 132 columns with an authentic K\&K definition. Specifically, A10a duplicates A10 (\citealt{winston1933}), C1-5a duplicates C1-5 (\citealt{firth1939}), and F4-6 duplicates F4-5 (an entry that Boroch's numbering mislabels as F4-6). Because none of these three adds an independent quotation, I exclude them from the network. The F4-5 quotation corresponds to \citealt{kluckhohnkelly1945a} (printed p. 87), yielding K\&K's F-IV-5. K\&K's printed definitions A-10a (\citealt{lowie1937}) and C-I-5a (\citealt{lynd1940}) draw their quotations from appendix entries A12 and C1-6.

\subsection{Quotations and citation keys}

Each analyzed definition is linked to a single quotation. Boroch supplies 170 of these quotations, comprising the 132 definitions from Boroch's Table 2 and the 39 appendix-only definitions, less the Sears addendum (C1-22), which attaches to a specialized commentary rather than a core definition. Definition D-III-4 lacks a Boroch entry; its quotation is taken from K\&K's printed footnote 14 (\citealt{roberts1951}, pp. 3 and 6, printed p. 60). Four quotations are not in English and are rendered from a translation table, with B-17 and C-I-18a translated from French, and F-I-5 and F-II-3 from German. C-I-18a adopts K\&K's own printed English translation, while the remaining three are working translations awaiting page-image verification.

Each definition is uniquely identified by a composite citation key combining Boroch's author, year, and page pinpoints with the opening six words of the quotation. The composite key is complete and unique across all 171 definitions. The page numbers reflect Boroch's printed citations and have not been independently verified against the primary volumes; the key therefore identifies each quotation as recorded in Boroch's compilation.

Because K\&K provide a single index entry per composite definition, each variant initially inherits a duplicate of its parent's sub-element links, totaling 41 ties. I then apply a lexical filter to assign 29 of these 41 ties to a specific variant. A link is retained on a variant if its quotation contains the sub-element's textual term and omitted from the other. In total, splitting the four composite entries expands the verified network from 167 to 171 definitions and from 1189 to 1201 edges.

\subsection{Checks, limitations, and open items}\label{app:checks}

The reconstructed networks depend on the transcription of K\&K's Index B and the verified-definition filtering criteria. Because element names are not unique across K\&K's taxonomic headings, identical sub-element labels are merged, which may occasionally link conceptually distinct uses. Several readings rely on OCR corrections recorded in the project's manual-fix tables. Two definitions omitted from Index A are included, namely C-I-18a and D-II-16, identified from main-text discussions. Conversely, K\&K's Part III statement codes (C-5, D-5, D-7, and E-17) are excluded, as they represent authorial commentary rather than definitions. The four split definitions and the 29 variant-assignment decisions represent interpretive judgments (Appendix~\ref{app:construction}); because the variants share a single printed index entry in K\&K, assigning concept ties based on quotation text is an analytical intervention that readers can evaluate directly against the quoted passages. The definition-level spanning trees and topic models reflect these structural decisions. Because projection tie weights frequently recur, maximum spanning trees are not unique. Betweenness centrality is therefore averaged across an ensemble of 1000 random trees; the trees illustrated in the figures depict the single representative member closest to the ensemble mean, such that local neighborhood topology reflects that specific realization, while ranks below the leading positions in Table~\ref{tab:mst-def-betweenness} exhibit sensitivity to tie-breaking.

Automated validation routines verify network topology, round-trip consistency, and attribute integrity, though they do not independently evaluate the substantive content of individual edges. Block validation places 1301 of the 1326 Index B edge rows (prior to the 34-row variant pruning) in their expected printed block, with 25 placed in adjacent blocks; page-image inspection confirmed 23 of those 25. Several items remain open for future archival reconciliation, including verifying the publication year of footnote 5 (attributed by Boroch to Linton 1945a) against high-resolution scans, resolving duplicate columns in the crosswalk, verifying the three non-English translations against primary editions, and confirming Boroch's page pinpoints directly against original source texts.
